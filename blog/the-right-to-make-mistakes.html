The Right to Make Mistakes

November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

I'm sure that at some point in your personal or professional life, you've made a mistake that left you feeling terrible. Perhaps it caused damage or a loss that couldn't be undone.

Maybe you accidentally deleted something important at work, transferred money to the wrong account, broke an expensive object, or made some other irreversible mistake.

When something like this happens to me, I sometimes find myself dwelling on it for a long time.

If only I had done that differently.

If only I had thought about it for two more seconds.

If only I had waited a little longer.

If only I hadn't gone there that day.

And then come a thousand other "if only" thoughts.

One of the first things that might cross my mind is: How am I going to tell everyone what happened?

I might start worrying about what my colleagues, friends, or the people around me will think of me.

Will they lose confidence in me? Will they think I'm incompetent? Will they judge me for what I've done?

I might even be tempted to hide the mistake, deny everything, or somehow cover my tracks so nobody knows I was responsible.

Or perhaps I'll start looking for excuses.

My computer wasn't working properly.

The tool malfunctioned.

I did everything correctly, but something else went wrong.

These are all natural human reactions when we realize we've made a serious mistake.

In this article, I'd like to talk about the right to make mistakes, particularly in the workplace.

It's a right we should give to others, and one that others should give to us.

The truth is that no living being capable of making choices can be expected to make every single decision correctly.

A conscious decision, an instinctive reaction, a choice made in a hurry, a statement driven by prejudice, or a message sent in anger — any of these can lead to an irreversible mistake that we deeply regret afterward.

When Things Go Wrong at Work

To make this more tangible, let's imagine a situation in a large organization.

Suppose you're responsible for an important part of a complex and sensitive system.

For whatever reason, you make a mistake that disrupts some of the organization's services and causes financial or operational damage.

What should you do?

Based on my experience and perspective, there are several things worth keeping in mind.

1. Accept Your Mistake and Report It Immediately

The first and most important step is to acknowledge what happened.

Accept, in your own mind, that you made a mistake, and inform the relevant people as quickly as possible.

You shouldn't have made that mistake in the first place. But now that it has happened, denying it or delaying the conversation won't make things better.

Regardless of the possible consequences for you personally, you need to communicate what happened.

The sooner the right people know about the problem, the sooner they can begin addressing it.

2. Tell the Truth

When reporting your mistake, be completely honest.

Explain exactly what happened, without hiding details, changing the story, or trying to make yourself look better.

Providing accurate information helps your colleagues, your team, and the organization make better decisions.

It allows them to understand the situation, prevent further damage, and take the most appropriate corrective actions.

In a critical situation, incomplete or misleading information can make an already difficult problem much worse.

3. Understand the Right to Make Mistakes

I believe people should have the right to make mistakes, and organizations should recognize that right.

However, this right has limits, depending on the nature of the responsibility and the potential consequences.

Consider a surgeon performing heart surgery. A mistake could seriously harm another person's health or cost them their life.

Or consider an air-defense system operator. A single incorrect decision could result in the deaths of many people.

The same applies to an operator at a nuclear power plant, where a serious mistake could lead to a human and environmental catastrophe.

In situations like these, there is practically no room for error.

This doesn't mean that the people performing these jobs are somehow incapable of making mistakes. It means that the consequences are so severe that every possible precaution must be taken to prevent them.

But in most professional responsibilities, there should be room for human error.

An accountant might calculate an amount incorrectly.

An IT engineer might accidentally damage or disrupt an infrastructure system.

These mistakes can be serious and costly, but they are also part of the reality of working with human beings.

4. You Are Not a Robot

You are human.

And being human means being capable of making mistakes.

An organization hires people, not robots.

By employing human beings, an organization must also accept that human error is part of the equation.

If someone expects absolutely no mistakes, they shouldn't expect that level of perfection from human employees.

People can be careful, experienced, knowledgeable, and responsible, and still make mistakes.

Recognizing this reality doesn't mean accepting carelessness. It means understanding the limitations of being human.

5. You Are Responsible

This is an important point.

Saying that you weren't responsible, that circumstances forced you to act that way, or that anyone else would have made the same mistake doesn't necessarily make the situation better.

In fact, these excuses can become additional mistakes on top of the original one.

When you accept a responsibility, you're expected to have the necessary training, knowledge, and expertise to perform that role.

You're expected to be prepared to make appropriate decisions, including under difficult or critical circumstances.

So if you make a mistake, you need to take responsibility for it.

Having the right to make mistakes doesn't mean being free from responsibility for them.

You made the mistake. Acknowledge it.

Taking responsibility is not the same as endlessly blaming yourself. It's about accepting your role in what happened and helping to make things right.

6. Stay Calm and Focused

Instead of getting trapped in self-blame, try to remain calm.

Focus your energy on understanding the situation, cooperating with others, and helping to resolve the problem as quickly as possible.

Constantly replaying the mistake in your mind won't undo it.

What matters now is what you can do to reduce the damage and improve the situation.

Your ability to stay focused and cooperate may be far more valuable than spending your time feeling guilty.

7. Learn from the Mistake

Every mistake should teach us something.

Take the time to understand what went wrong, why it happened, and what could prevent a similar situation in the future.

Document your findings.

Share the experience with your colleagues.

Make sure others can learn from what happened so that, when they face a similar situation, they can respond more effectively.

A mistake becomes much more valuable when its lessons are shared.

What If Someone Else Makes the Mistake?

Now let's look at the situation from the other side.

What if the person who made the mistake is your colleague, an employee, or someone you're responsible for managing?

I believe there are a few important principles we should follow.

1. Don't Blame Them

Blaming someone who has already made a mistake rarely helps.

In many cases, it only makes the situation worse.

The person probably already knows that something went wrong and may be experiencing considerable stress, anxiety, or guilt.

Adding more pressure won't necessarily help solve the problem.

Instead of focusing on who deserves blame, focus on what needs to be done.

2. Trust Them

Interestingly, the person who made the mistake may also be the person best positioned to help fix it.

They often have the most immediate knowledge of what happened and how the system or process was affected.

Try to restore their confidence.

Reduce unnecessary tension.

Create an environment where they feel comfortable explaining the situation and participating in the recovery process.

Trust can make a significant difference in how effectively a team responds to a crisis.

3. Take Responsibility as a Manager

If you're managing the person who made the mistake, then from the organization's perspective, you also carry responsibility for their work.

You should support them and help manage the situation.

Don't disappear when things go wrong.

Don't distance yourself from the problem just to protect your own position.

As a manager or team leader, you should stand behind every member of your team.

Leadership isn't just about taking credit when things go well.

It's also about accepting responsibility and supporting your people when things go wrong.

4. Recognize the Value of Mistakes

Imagine an employee makes a mistake that costs the organization 100 units of money.

You could look at the situation and say that the company has lost 100 units.

But there's another way to look at it.

You now have an employee who has gained experience through a mistake that cost 100 units.

That experience has value.

Of course, the financial loss is real, and the mistake shouldn't be repeated.

But if the organization learns from what happened, that experience can help prevent much greater losses in the future.

Let the rest of the team learn from it.

An expensive mistake becomes even more expensive when nobody learns anything from it.

Progress Is Built on Experience, Including Mistakes

Think about modern medicine.

Today, surgeons can perform extraordinarily complex procedures, including open-heart surgery and heart transplantation.

These achievements didn't happen overnight.

They are the result of generations of research, experimentation, accumulated knowledge, and, tragically, many failures and lost lives along the way.

Medical science has evolved by studying both successes and failures.

The experiences of previous generations have helped make procedures safer and more effective for those who came after them.

The same principle applies to history.

One of the most valuable reasons to study history is to recognize patterns in human decisions and their consequences.
