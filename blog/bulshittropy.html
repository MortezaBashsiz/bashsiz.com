Bulshittropy

On the Drift Toward Absurdity and the Erosion of Authenticity

November 13, 2026 · 9 min read

“Perhaps the world is not losing its meaning. Perhaps we have simply produced so many counterfeit meanings that we can no longer recognize the real ones.”

1. The Second Law of Absurdity

The second law of thermodynamics tells us that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. Left to itself, such a system tends toward states of greater entropy. Order does not sustain itself without the expenditure of energy.

Perhaps something analogous is happening in the world of thought, culture, and human relationships.

Perhaps, just as maintaining physical order requires energy, preserving meaning, authenticity, and intellectual depth demands continuous effort. And just as physical systems may deteriorate when left unattended, culture, when surrendered entirely to the mechanisms of production and consumption, may gradually drift toward triviality.

I call this tendency Bulshittropy.

A combination of bullshit and entropy: the entropy of bullshit.

Bulshittropy describes a process in which the volume of meaningless, superficial, imitative, and inauthentic things grows faster than our capacity to recognize, question, and discard them.

It is not a physical law. Nor does it pretend to be a scientific one. It is a philosophical metaphor for something we increasingly experience but have yet to find adequate language to describe.

The world is becoming saturated with content, but not necessarily enriched with meaning.

We write more, speak more, publish more, and express more opinions than ever before.

But do we understand more?

2. When Appearance Replaces Substance

One of the defining symptoms of Bulshittropy is that things gradually become less concerned with what they are and more concerned with what they appear to be.

People no longer merely live their lives; they manufacture representations of them.

Books are not always written to communicate ideas. Sometimes they are written to resemble important books.

Theories are not always constructed to explain reality. Sometimes they exist merely to attach an impressive new name to something painfully obvious.

And people do not necessarily speak in order to understand. Sometimes they speak to occupy the position of someone who understands.

There is a subtle but fundamental difference between knowing and performing knowledge, between thinking and performing thought, between being authentic and performing authenticity.

In a world afflicted by Bulshittropy, these distinctions gradually dissolve.

The appearance of thought can replace thought itself, because manufacturing the appearance of intelligence is generally easier than the difficult act of thinking.

Producing a profound-sounding sentence may require nothing more than a handful of abstract words. Arriving at a genuinely profound idea, however, may demand years of reading, experience, doubt, and failure.

The problem begins when both are perceived as equally valuable.

Or worse, when the counterfeit becomes more successful precisely because it is simpler, more attractive, and easier to consume.

3. The Industrialization of Bullshit

Bullshit has always existed.

Throughout history, people have lied, invented baseless theories, produced worthless works, and spoken with astonishing confidence about subjects they barely understood.

The problem, therefore, is not that humanity has suddenly become stupid.

The problem is that we have learned to industrialize bullshit.

In the past, even producing a worthless book required a certain investment of time, money, and human effort. Today, within minutes, one can generate a text that possesses all the superficial characteristics of a serious intellectual work without necessarily containing a single serious thought.

Technology has nearly eliminated the distance between having something to say and publishing something.

And this achievement, despite its extraordinary emancipatory potential, carries a darker consequence:

We no longer need to have something to say in order to speak endlessly.

In such a world, silence becomes an anomaly.

Someone who knows nothing is rarely compelled to remain silent. There is always an opinion to express, an article to publish, an image to generate, or an interpretation to offer.

And when everything is being said, hearing what genuinely deserves to be said becomes increasingly difficult.

4. The Slow Death of Authenticity

Authenticity is a difficult concept.

No idea emerges entirely independently of the past. No artist exists outside the history of art. No writer writes without being influenced by language and the works of others.

Authenticity, therefore, cannot simply mean creating something out of nothing.

Perhaps authenticity is better understood through the relationship between a person and what they create.

An authentic work is not necessarily one that resembles nothing else. It is one that emerges from a genuine encounter with the world.

From lived experience, a serious question, an inner necessity, or an honest attempt to understand.

Yet in the world of Bulshittropy, inner necessity gradually gives way to external obligation.

The writer writes because they must publish.

The artist creates because they must be seen.

The intellectual constructs theories because they must have something new to say.

And the individual manufactures an identity because they must remain legible to others.

What disappears in this process is not necessarily aesthetic quality.

Quite the opposite.

Cultural products may become increasingly beautiful, polished, professional, and technically flawless.

And yet something essential may be missing from within them.

Something that cannot easily be measured, optimized by an algorithm, or reproduced through imitation.

The necessity of their existence.

5. The Attention Economy and the Triumph of Superficiality

In a world where human attention is limited and the volume of produced content is practically limitless, competition is no longer exclusively about truth or quality.

It is about visibility.

And visibility does not necessarily correlate with value.

A simple, confident, emotionally provocative claim can spread far more rapidly than a careful, complex, and qualified explanation.

Truth often requires conditions. It requires context, exceptions, and uncertainty.

Bullshit, on the other hand, can speak with absolute certainty.

It has no obligation to endure the complexity of reality.

The result is an environment in which excessive simplification becomes a competitive advantage.

This does not mean that simplicity itself is worthless. The ability to express a complex idea clearly and simply is an intellectual virtue.

The problem begins when simplicity is no longer the product of deep understanding but a substitute for it.

When short answers replace difficult questions.

And when the confidence of the speaker becomes more important than the accuracy of what is being said.

6. Machines That Never Stop Talking

Artificial intelligence introduces Bulshittropy into an entirely new phase.

For the first time, the mass production of coherent texts, visually impressive images, and seemingly credible arguments no longer necessarily requires a human being to invest substantial thought or effort into each individual creation.

This technology can be an extraordinary instrument for science, art, and intellectual exploration.

But it can also make the production of things that merely resemble science, art, and thought dramatically easier.

The greatest danger is not that machines produce bullshit.

Human beings have been remarkably proficient at that for thousands of years.

The danger is that we can now manufacture bullshit at unprecedented speed, with convincing appearances and negligible cost.

We may even reach a point where distinguishing a text born from years of reflection from one generated in seconds becomes impossible through examination of the finished product alone.

Under such circumstances, the essential question is no longer merely:

“Is this text correct?”

We must also ask:

Who is responsible for this claim?

What process brought it into existence?

And is there anyone who genuinely stands behind these words?

7. Is This Theory Itself Bullshit?

Here, we must confront an uncomfortable contradiction.

If the world is indeed drifting toward Bulshittropy, what guarantees that the concept of Bulshittropy itself is not merely another manifestation of the very phenomenon it attempts to criticize?

Is inventing an English-sounding term, attaching a scientific suffix to it, and using it to explain a broad cultural anxiety not precisely the sort of intellectual performance this essay condemns?

Am I not simply manufacturing an attractive theory to explain something that may be far more complicated than I am willing to admit?

Possibly.

And that possibility deserves to be taken seriously.

Because one of the most dangerous characteristics of bullshit is its inability to doubt itself.

A worthwhile theory must be capable of tolerating the possibility that it is wrong.

Perhaps the world is not becoming more superficial.

Perhaps our increased access to cultural production merely exposes us to a larger portion of the mediocrity that has always existed.

Perhaps we judge the past through its surviving masterpieces while judging the present through everything produced on an ordinary Tuesday.

Perhaps authenticity has not disappeared. Perhaps it has simply become more difficult to find.

And perhaps Bulshittropy is not a law describing the deterioration of civilization, but a name for our growing crisis of discernment.

Yet even if that is true, the problem remains.

For a human being buried beneath an endless accumulation of information, claims, images, and theories, the difference between the absence of meaning and the inability to find it may eventually become practically indistinguishable.

8. Resisting Entropy

If Bulshittropy is understood as a metaphor for culture's tendency toward triviality, resisting it requires energy.

Understanding is difficult.

Creating is difficult.

Remaining authentic is difficult.

Admitting ignorance is difficult.

And perhaps most difficult of all is refusing to speak when one has nothing meaningful to say.

The modern world has given us countless tools to produce more, but it rarely asks whether what we produce has any reason to exist.

Perhaps resistance to Bulshittropy begins with that question.

Not with the invention of another theory.

Not with another book about the crisis of meaning.

Not even with the publication of this essay.

But with the ability to stop.

With reading one book instead of consuming a hundred summaries.

With contemplating one question instead of possessing answers to a thousand.

With accepting that not everything requires our opinion.

And with the courage not to create something that has no genuine reason to exist.

Conclusion: A World Full of Nothing

Perhaps the tragedy of our age is not that valuable things are no longer being created.

There are still people who think, write, discover, and produce works worthy of years of attention.

Perhaps the tragedy is that their work floats in an ocean of unnecessary things, an ocean that expands with every passing moment.

We have arrived at a peculiar point in history.

Never before have we possessed such extraordinary capabilities for producing knowledge, and never before has the ability to manufacture something that merely resembles knowledge been so widely accessible.

Never before have the tools of artistic creation been so widespread, and never before has reproducing the appearance of art been so effortless.

The world is overflowing with words, images, sounds, and ideas.

But abundance is not the same as richness.

And perhaps this is the ultimate destination of Bulshittropy:

Not a world in which nothing has meaning, but a world in which everything claims to have meaning.

A world in which truth does not disappear, but becomes buried beneath so many convincing imitations that no one has the patience to search for it anymore.
